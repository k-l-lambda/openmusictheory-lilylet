# Strengthening Endings with V⁷

> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - This chapter introduces how composers add a seventh to the dominant to strengthen its pull toward the tonic. There are three ways a V<sup>7</sup> can resolve to tonic:
> 	- **The default resolution:** where all active notes resolve according to their tendencies.
> 	- **Incomplete V<sup>7</sup>:** where the fifth is omitted from the V<sup>7</sup> and the V<sup>7</sup>‘s root is doubled instead to create a complete tonic.
> 	- **Leading-tone drop:** where the leading tone in a complete V<sup>7</sup> leaps down to *sol* ($\hat{5}$) to create a complete tonic.

The phrase in Example 1 ends with a perfect authentic cadence (PAC) similar to those we saw and wrote in the [Introduction to Harmony, Cadences, and Endings](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/endings-1/) chapter. Here, however, the V chord contains an extra note, *fa* ($\hat{4}$), that transforms it from a major triad into a dominant seventh chord. Since the seventh adds dissonance (and therefore instability) to the chord, it strengthens the pull of the V chord toward I. Compare the sound of the excerpts in Example 2.

## The Default Resolution of V<sup>7</sup> to I

When the notes of a complete V<sup>7</sup> chord resolve according to their typical tendencies, we end up with a tonic triad that has three roots and one third—no fifth (**Examples 3a** and **3b**). This is a completely normal, expected, and common resolution of V<sup>7</sup> to I. It’s okay to leave out the fifth here since it doesn’t provide essential information about the chord. By contrast, the root and third are essential: the root determines the chord’s name, and the third determines its quality.

The resolution in **Example 3c**, where *re* ($\hat{2}$) goes up to *mi* ($\hat{3}$), is very uncommon, and such an unusual doubling in the tonic chord (two roots, two thirds) usually leads to voice-leading problems. We suggest avoiding this kind of resolution.

🎼 [MuseScore score](https://musescore.com/user/32728834/scores/6224872)

Example 3. Default resolution of V<sup>7</sup>.

To resolve V<sup>7</sup> to I using a default resolution:

1. Write the entire bass: *sol* to *do* ($\hat{5}$–$\hat{1}$)
2. Write the entire soprano by choosing an active note to place in the soprano over V<sup>7</sup>, then resolving that note according to its tendency. Example 4 shows the tendencies for active notes in V<sup>7</sup>.
	1. If you are writing in minor, remember to use *ti* ($\uparrow\hat{7}$), not *te* ($\downarrow\hat{7}$) in V<sup>7</sup> to make the chord major!
3. Fill in the inner voices by asking “what do I have, what do I need, and what is the tendency of those notes?”

| Note in V7 | Resolution in I (or i) |
| --- | --- |
| *fa* ($\hat{4}$) | *mi/me* ($\hat{3}$) |
| *re* ($\hat{2}$) | *do* ($\hat{1}$) |
| *ti* ($\hat{7}$) | *do* ($\hat{1}$) |

Example 4. Tendencies of active notes in V<sup>7</sup>.

## Alternative Resolutions of V<sup>7</sup> to I

Sometimes a composer may want the tonic chord to be complete rather than the incomplete chord that occurs in the default resolution. Two alternative resolutions of V<sup>7</sup> make it possible to create a complete I. Note that the steps for writing remain the same as for the default resolution: write the entire bass, write the entire soprano choosing an active note over V<sup>7</sup>, then fill in the inner voices together as a pair by asking “what do I have, what do I need, and where do those notes go?”

#### Incomplete V<sup>7</sup>

**Examples 5a** and **5b** show a resolution where the fifth has been omitted from the V<sup>7</sup>, allowing that chord to resolve to a complete I. When we omit the fifth, we need to select a note to double in order to retain our four-voice texture. The only note we can double is the root, since doubling *ti* ($\uparrow\hat{7}$) or *fa* ($\hat{4}$) would create parallel octaves when both voices resolve according to their tendencies (**Examples 5c** and **5d**).

#### Leading-Tone Drop

It’s also possible to create a complete I from a complete V<sup>7</sup>, but in order to do so, we have to allow a tendency tone to move somewhere other than its expected resolution. Example 6 shows how this is possible using a leading-tone drop. [^1] Here, *ti* ($\uparrow\hat{7}$) drops down to *sol* ($\hat{5}$) in an inner voice. This works because: (1) *ti* ($\uparrow\hat{7}$) is not a dissonant note; (2) *ti* ($\uparrow\hat{7}$) is in an inner voice, so it’s not too noticeable; and (3) *ti* ($\uparrow\hat{7}$) is the closest note that can move to *sol* ($\hat{5}$) in the tonic without causing parallels.

🎼 [MuseScore score](https://musescore.com/user/32728834/scores/6224804)

Example 6. Alternative resolution of V<sup>7</sup> involving a leading-tone drop.

Two things are worth emphasizing here:

1. Use the leading-tone drop only in the alto or tenor voice to hide the fact that it’s not resolving as expected.
2. The leading tone always leaps down to *sol* ($\hat{5}$), never to *mi* ($\hat{3}$), which is further away than *sol* ($\hat{5}$).

## Summary

Example 7 summarizes the three possible ways to resolve V<sup>7</sup>–I. You may notice that we have not discussed how V<sup>7</sup> works in half cadences (HCs). V<sup>7</sup> is much less common in a HC since the HC is already unstable, and adding a seventh makes it that much more unstable. Since such a cadence more often appears in Romantic music, Janet Schmalfeldt (2011, 202) has termed HCs involving V<sup>7</sup> the “19<sup>th</sup>-century HC.”
