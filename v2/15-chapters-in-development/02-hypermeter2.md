# Hypermeter (new version)

> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - Hypermeter is a perceived (non-notated) metric organization that is at a conceptually higher level than the regular meter.
> - Hypermeter exists on a continuum: in some music, it may be weak or non-existent; in other music (e.g., most dance music), it may be strong and clear.
> - Quadruple is the most common type of hypermeter.
> - Hyperbeats can have associations with musical features:
> 	- Hyperbeat 1: Associated with beginnings
> 	- Hyperbeat 4: Associated with endings/cadences
> 	- Hyperbeats 2 & 3: Associated with middles
> - A regular hypermeter can be disrupted by reinterpretation and repetition.

## Overview

The prefix “hyper-” means “over” or “above.” Hypermeter is a perceived (non-notated) metric organization that is at a conceptually higher level above regular meters. Even in notated music, hypermeter is *not* notated—it is something you hear when you detect regular spans of time longer than a single measure.

Hypermeter is reliably found in dance music. For example, in Rihanna’s 2012 song “Diamonds,” the four-measure looping harmonic progression (G–Bmi–A–A) articulates the hypermeter. Each repetition of the chord loop begins a new hypermeasure—listen for them beginning on the lyrics “Find light in the beautiful …”, “You’re a shooting star I see …”, “I knew that we’d become …”, and so on. In Example 1, the lowest row of colored arches shows the hypermeasures, each consisting of four hyperbeats (measures).

Like regular meters, hypermeters may be quadruple, triple, or duple:

- Quadruple: four measures (hyperbeats) = one hypermeasure
- Triple: three measures (hyperbeats) = one hypermeasure
- Duple: two measures (hyperbeats) = one hypermeasure

Quadruple is by far the most common hypermeter, duple is less common, and triple is the least common. Hypermeasures are typically perceived due to changes and/or repetitions that take place during that span of time. In “Diamonds,” this was the repetition of the four-bar chord loop, but there are other methods as well. If a passage contains a four-measure phrase and its immediate repetition, the passage can be heard as two quadruple hypermeasures. Similarly, a four-measure phrase followed by a distinctly contrasting four-measure phrase can produce two quadruple hypermeasures.

Like regular meter, hypermeter tends to be mostly consistent throughout a work (e.g., always quadruple), but irregular lengths can occur, typically due to [phrase expansion](../04-form/04-expansion-and-contraction.md) techniques that result in hypermetric disruption, discussed more [below](#hypermetric-disruptions-reinterpretation-and-repetition). If lengths are very irregular throughout, a piece or a passage of music may not have a perceivable hypermeter. And as always, ambiguities often arise in music, and different interpretations of a piece’s hypermeter are possible.

### Hyperbeat Associations

When listening for regular meter, certain musical features are associated with certain beats, and this orients us within the meter. For example, chord changes usually happen on beat 1; a backbeat happens on beats 2 and 4. Similarly, in hypermeter, you can often hear which hyperbeat is happening if you know what to listen for. In quadruple hypermeter, you can listen for the following things:

- **Hyperbeat 1:** Associated with beginnings. Can be the beginning of a new phrase or subphrase or it can be a repetition of one you’ve already heard.
- **Hyperbeat 4:** Associated with endings. In music with cadences, the cadences typically happen in hyperbeat 4.
- **Hyperbeats 2 and 3:** Associated with middles. These hyperbeats are often are not as easily identifiable as hyperbeats 1 and 4. In music with cadences, hyperbeat 3 may feel like it’s leading toward the cadence in hyperbeat 4.

## Examples of Hypermeter

### Rihanna, “Diamonds”: Hypermeter and phrasing

We introduced hypermeter with “Diamonds” (Example 1) because of its clear, regular, quadruple hypermeter. But the way the phrasing lines up with the hypermeter is worth further attention.

Notice how in the verses (Example 2a), Rihanna’s phrases start in the first hyperbeat of each hypermeasure, but they *don’t* start on the metrical downbeat. But at the very beginning of each chorus (Example 2b), her lyrical phrase contains a pick-up that does lead into the simultaneous arrival of the metrical and hypermetrical downbeats. The alignment of metrical downbeat, hyperdownbeat, and melodic accent emphasize the arrival of the chorus.

### The Beatles, “A Hard Day’s Night” (1964): Hypermetric repetition

This song also contains quadruple hypermeter. To hear it, pay attention to the beginnings of each lyric phrase (“It’s been a hard …”, “But when I get home …”, “When I’m home …” ) and of each harmonic progression (I–IV–I–♭VII–I, or G–C–G–F–G, for the first two phrases). Notice that the quadruple hypermeter is very consistent throughout the song, except for the very last phrase.

In Example 3, the last phrase has the hypermetric counts $\overbrace{1-2-3-4-\mathbf{3-4-3-4}}$ where we might have expected another repetition of a regular $1-2-3-4$ hypermetric counting. In this last phrase, the music associated with the 3rd and 4th hyperbeats is repeating, which leads to a perception of repeating those two hyperbeats instead of starting over with the material for hyperbeat 1 (Hypermetric Repetition). To hear this interpretation, compare the last phrase of each A section and pay special attention to the lines that end with “feel all right” or “feel okay.” They only happen one time per A section, but in the last one, it keeps repeating instead of moving on to something else like it had been doing.

Labeling the final phrases as two hypermeasures $(\overbrace{1-2-3-4}\quad\overbrace{1-2-3-4})$ is also an accurate interpretation of the passage, but the advantage of labeling the repeated 3–4s is that the hypermetrical analysis better reflects the musical repetition.

Examples 5–7 are interactive examples that present the music in Example 4 at three different tempos. Listen to each of the different versions (click on each image) and try to see what tactus you entrain to at each tempo by conducting the meter with your arm. It’s likely to be different especially between the double-speed and half-speed versions:

- Example 5: This is performed at the normal, notated tempo for this piece. You probably conduct a simple triple meter, as notated. You can likely feel the four-bar hypermeter easily as well.
- Example 6: This version is twice as fast as normal. At this tempo, you probably shift your tactus to the hyperbeat and conduct as if this were in compound quadruple or compound duple meter. Effectively, you are conducting the hypermeter instead of the notated meter. The hypermeter is therefore very easy to perceive—possibly easier than the notated meter.
- Example 7: This version is twice as slow as normal. At this tempo, your tactus may feel almost uncomfortably slow. Because the tactus and beat are so slow, it may be very difficult to perceive the hypermeter notated in Example 4—the spans of time are too long to entrain to.

If you struggle to hear hypermeter (in this example or any example), remember that hypermeter is a perceptible and interpretational phenomenon, and your perception of it can change depending on the tempo. Just as you can hear different levels of meter at these different tempos, you can hear different levels of meter even if the tempo does not change. The perceptual change in tactus you may experience is just your mind entraining different levels of meter.

## Hypermetric Disruptions: reinterpretation and repetition

### Reinterpretation

Hypermetric reinterpretation is a type of hypermetric disruption that occurs when a single hyperbeat is simultaneously functioning in two ways. Almost always, this occurs at a phrase elision, in which the end of one hypermeasure (hyperbeat 4) is simultaneously the beginning of a new hypermeasure (hyperbeat 1).[^1] In an analysis, we notate this with an equals sign: $4=1$. An example of two hypermeasures with reinterpretation would be $1-2-3-\mathbf{4=1}-2-3-4$.

As an example, take Wolfgang Mozart’s Piano Concerto no. 21, K. 467, mvt. i (1785). In Example 8, in measure 12, the end of the first phrase elides with the beginning of the second phrase. Note that the notation $4=1$ in the measure where the hypermetric reinterpretation occurs. The music from the third hypermeasure is just like that of the second hypermeasure, so the second one functions as a model for how the third should go. Listen to how measure 12 still functions as the end of the first phrase, but the new phrase simultaneously takes over, providing a clear sense of the beginning of a new hypermeasure. ### Repetition

Hypermetric repetition is another type of hypermetric disruption that indicates a hypermeasure has been expanded beyond its expected number of measures (from four measures to five, for example). If you say you hear a hypermetric repetition, this means you think one or more hyperbeats within a hypermeasure have been immediately repeated. For example, instead of $1-2-3-4$, you could have hyperbeat 4 repeat $(1-2-3-4-\mathbf{4})$, or 3 and 4 could repeat together $(1-2-3-4-\mathbf{3-4})$ like in “Hard Day’s Night.” Hypermetric repetition is usually most convincing when the musical material in question is a variant of a previously stated passage, because similar music should normally be interpreted similarly. Hypermetric repetition is a type of hypermetric disruption.

In Mozart’s duet “Là ci darem la mano” from *Don Giovanni*, Don Giovanni sings a parallel period; immediately after, Zerlina sings essentially the same parallel period (with different lyrics), but this time the last phrase is expanded. Compare Don Giovanni’s consequent phrase with Zerlina’s: because we hear Don Giovanni’s phrase first, it serves as the model of what Zerlina’s will sound like, and Zerlina’s version then violates our expectations, providing a mild musical surprise that extends the phrase from the expected four measures to six. Example 9 shows the expansion as a hypermetric repetition where hyperbeats 3 and 4 are immediately repeated. They are slightly varied, but their cadential function and overall effect remain very similar. The notation CAD indicates the location of the cadence that was proposed and then evaded in favor the PAC that is proposed and accepted two measures later.
