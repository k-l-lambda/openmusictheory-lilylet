# Pop Harmonic Schemas

## Summary

OMT 2e treats pop harmony as a set of stock chord progressions (schemas). They are often looped, especially in choruses. A schema stays recognizable under rotation, chord substitution, chromatic inflection and inversion. The families are blues-based (plagal), four-chord (I, IV, V, vi), classical (lament, circle of fifths), puff, and modal (mixolydian, aeolian, dorian, lydian). Pentatonic harmony and fragile, absent and emergent tonics cover progressions whose mode or tonic is ambiguous.

## Master Table

All spellings use a C tonic. Every chord was checked against the stated mode (see Constraints 1–2).

| Schema | Roman numerals | In C | Mode / context | Characteristic line or feature | Typical location |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plagal | IV–I | F–C | major | 5–6–5 shuffle neighbor (fifth of I → sixth) | verse vamp (R&B/soul) |
| Plagal sigh | IV–iv–I | F–Fm–C | major + borrowed iv | *la–le–sol* (6̂–↓6̂–5̂) | phrase endings |
| Double plagal | ♭VII–IV–I | B♭–F–C | mixolydian | IV/IV–IV–I: two plagal fourths | coda/loop ("Hey Jude" coda) |
| Extended plagal | (♭VI–)♭III–♭VII–IV–I | (A♭–)E♭–B♭–F–C | chain of applied IVs | roots descend by P4 | verse loop |
| Doo-wop | I–vi–IV–V | C–Am–F–G | major | tonic approached by authentic V–I | looped, verse and chorus |
| Singer/songwriter | vi–IV–I–V or I–V–vi–IV (minor: i–VI–III–VII) | Am–F–C–G | major/relative-minor ambiguous | major tonic approached plagally; no V–I | looped |
| Hopscotch | IV–V–vi–I (minor: VI–VII–i–III) | F–G–Am–C | major/relative-minor ambiguous | roots step–step–skip; major tonic approached by skip | looped (since c. 2010) |
| Lament | i–♭VII–♭VI–V | Cm–B♭–A♭–G | minor | bass *do–te–le–sol* (1̂–↓7̂–↓6̂–5̂) | looped phrase, turnaround on V(7) |
| Circle of fifths | i–iv–VII–III or vi–ii–V–I | Cm–Fm–B♭–E♭ / Am–Dm–G–C | minor → relative major | roots descend P5, 4+ chords | can modulate to relative major |
| Puff | I–iii–IV | C–Em–F | major | bass 1̂–3̂–4̂, 3̂ harmonized as root-position iii | phrase openings, not looped |
| Subtonic shuttle | I–♭VII (or ♭VII–I) | C–B♭ | mixolydian | melody stresses *te* (↓7̂) | intro/verse loop |
| Subtonic shuttle (aeolian) | i–♭VII | Cm–B♭ | aeolian | same as above, minor tonic | intro/verse loop |
| Aeolian shuttle | i–♭VII–♭VI–♭VII | Cm–B♭–A♭–B♭ | aeolian | i↔♭VI shuttle with passing ♭VII | looped, chorus |
| Aeolian cadence | ♭VI–♭VII–i, usually ♭VI–♭VII–I | A♭–B♭–Cm / A♭–B♭–C | aeolian, often picardy | goal-directed, not looped | phrase ending, fanfare |
| Dorian shuttle | i–IV | Cm–F | dorian | upper-voice neighbor stressing *la* (↑6̂) | pervasive loop (funk, disco) |
| Lydian shuttle | I–II♯ | C–D | lydian (brief) | *fi* (↑4̂); II♯ often over tonic pedal | opening; later neutralized |
| Lydian cadence | II♯–IV–I | D–F–C | lydian → major | chromatic *fi–fa–mi* (↑4̂–4̂–3̂) | looped |

Notation: II♯ and III♯ are major II and III chords (the sharp marks the raised third). In modal contexts v2 writes ♭VI and ♭VII even where they are diatonic, to avoid confusion with vii°. In relative-minor readings of the four-chord schemas it writes plain VI, VII and III.

## Blues-Based (Plagal) Schemas

All are "flat-side" schemas built on plagal IV–I motion. The other chords (♭VII as IV/IV, minor iv) embellish that motion.

- **I–IV vamp**: likely derived from the 5–6 shuffle-blues guitar pattern. Examples: Sam and Dave, "Soul Man" (verse); Wilson Pickett, "In the Midnight Hour" (verse); Neil Young, "After the Gold Rush" (opening).
- **Plagal sigh** (term from J. Kent Williams and Frank Lehman): IV–iv–I with *la–le–sol*. The semitone *le–sol* pulls strongly to tonic. Lyrics are usually nostalgic or sentimental. Example: Green Day, "Wake Me Up When September Ends."
- **Double plagal** (Walter Everett's term): ♭VII–IV–I = IV/IV–IV–I. With a major tonic and *te* it is also a mixolydian schema. Example: the Beatles, "Hey Jude" (coda).
- **Extended plagal**: applied IVs used in sequence, like the classical descending-fifths sequence (here roots fall by fourths): ♭VI–♭III–♭VII–IV–I. Example: Jimi Hendrix, "Hey Joe" (verse, in E: C–G–D–A–E). It also reads as pentatonic; see below.
- **12-bar and 16-bar blues**: see [Blues Progressions](blues-progressions.md). v2's summary variants are dominant sevenths on every chord, IV in m. 2, ii–V in mm. 13–14 for V–IV, an applied ii–V in m. 8 (jazz blues), and a turnaround in mm. 15–16.

## Four-Chord Schemas

All three use only I, IV, V and vi and are usually looped, so the starting chord is not diagnostic.

| Schema | Prototype | Variants (v2) | Examples (v2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doo-wop | I–vi–IV–V | ii for IV (I–vi–ii–V); rotation IV–V–I–vi | Gene Chandler, "Duke of Earl"; Rebecca Black, "Friday"; Bonnie Tyler, "Total Eclipse of the Heart" (chorus); Bruce Springsteen, "Hungry Heart" (ii for IV); Coldplay, "Viva la Vida" (rotation from IV) |
| Singer/songwriter | vi–IV–I–V and I–V–vi–IV (equally common) | rotation IV–I–V–vi ("deceptive" variant ending V–vi); V–vi–IV–I | Luis Fonsi and Daddy Yankee, "Despacito" (Bm–G–D–A, D major or B minor); Heart, "What About Love"; Lady Gaga, "Alejandro" (chorus, IV–I–V–vi) |
| Hopscotch | IV–V–vi–I / VI–VII–i–III | V replaces VII in minor: VI–V–i–III (V brings *ti* for *te*); V⁶ keeps the step-step-skip bass | Sam Smith, "Dancing with a Stranger"; DJ Khaled, Justin Bieber and Quavo, "No Brainer"; BLACKPINK, "Love to Hate Me" (VI–V–i–III); YNW Melly, "Mixed Personalities" (inverted V) |

### Telling them apart by ear (v2 rule)

Listen to how the major tonic is approached. The approach to the minor tonic does not help, because singer/songwriter and hopscotch both reach it by step.

| Approach to major I | Schema |
|---|---|
| V–I (authentic) | doo-wop |
| IV–I (plagal) | singer/songwriter |
| vi–I (skip, no traditional cadence) | hopscotch |

### Tonal ambiguity

Singer/songwriter and hopscotch have no authentic cadence, so major I and relative-minor i are both plausible tonics. The tonic is decided by the surrounding progressions, which chord begins and ends the cycle, and the melody's important pitches. Songs exploit this to pivot between relative keys. Heart's "What About Love" has an intro in D minor (i–VII–VI–VII over a tonic pedal), an ambiguous verse (Dm–B♭–F–C) and a chorus in F major (I⁶–IV–V).

## Classical Schemas in Pop

### Lament

- The bass descends the minor tetrachord *do–te–le–sol* (1̂–↓7̂–↓6̂–5̂). The diatonic-triad harmonization is i–♭VII–♭VI–V.
- *te* can be harmonized several ways. Muse, "Thoughts of a Dying Atheist," uses a second-inversion B♭ chord (III⁶₄, or VII with two upper neighbors).
- Ch08 variants: minor v instead of V (aeolian lament, i–♭VII–♭VI–v, as in Eva Simons, "I Don't Like You"); major I instead of i; passing V⁶ between i and ♭VII, or IV⁶ between ♭VII and ♭VI.
- Dire Straits, "Sultans of Swing": the middle two chords are syncopated and shortened to make room for V7 as the turnaround.
- Classical background: the ground bass of Purcell's "Dido's Lament" and Bach's "Crucifixus" (B-minor Mass).

### Circle of fifths

- Four or more chords with roots descending by P5. It need not start on tonic.
- It usually starts on minor i and ends on the relative major (i–iv–VII–III). VII–III sounds like V–I in the relative major, so the schema can modulate there.
- Chord qualities can change to make applied dominants or chains of applied ii–V's (Radiohead, "You and Whose Army?", E major or C♯ minor).
- Examples: Aqua, "Barbie Girl" (chorus); Muse, "Thoughts of a Dying Atheist" (verse, lament then circle of fifths, leading to a chorus in the relative major).

## Puff Schemas

The puff is a goal-directed phrase opener, not a loop. The chord after IV varies.

| Form | Numerals | Example (v2) |
|---|---|---|
| Prototype | I–iii–IV (then I) | Peter, Paul and Mary, "Puff, the Magic Dragon" (A–C♯m–D–A) |
| Prototype → V | I–iii⁷–IV–V¹¹ | Marvin Gaye, "Let's Get It On" (E♭–Gm7–A♭–A♭/B♭) |
| Minor | i–III–iv; also with major IV | the Animals, "House of the Rising Sun" (Am–C–D–F, i–III–IV–VI) |
| Chromatic | I–III♯–IV, inner line *sol–si–la* (5̂–♯5̂–6̂) | Radiohead, "Creep" (G–B–C–Cm: puff + plagal sigh) |

- III♯ can act as V/vi. III♯–vi is an authentic resolution, and III♯–IV is then a deceptive one (in A minor terms, V–VI).
- C–E–Am–F is a singer/songwriter variant with V/vi in place of V.
- MGMT, "Weekend Wars": V⁶/vi–vi–V⁶–I (A/C♯–Dm–C/E–F) sets up A–B♭, a deceptive III♯–IV.

## Modal Schemas

A modal schema can appear without the whole song being in that mode. Each mode is defined by one color note relative to major or natural minor.

| Mode | Tonic | Color note | Signature chord(s) | Function of signature chord |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mixolydian | major | *te* (↓7̂) | ♭VII, v (iii° unused) | ♭VII = dominant (substitute for V) |
| Aeolian | minor | *le*, *te* (↓6̂, ↓7̂) | ♭VI, ♭VII | ♭VI = subdominant; ♭VII = dominant |
| Dorian | minor | *la* (↑6̂) | major IV, minor ii (vi° unused) | IV = usually subdominant |
| Lydian | major | *fi* (↑4̂) | II♯ (iv° and vii unused) | II♯ = subdominant or dominant (dominant in the shuttle: *fi*→*sol*) |

Modal chords keep the function of their diatonic counterparts (ch13 Example 2). If II♯ goes to V, analyze it as V/V. Label it II♯ only when it goes elsewhere.

### Mixolydian

- Double plagal ♭VII–IV–I (above).
- **Subtonic shuttle** I–♭VII / ♭VII–I. The melody uses *te* as embellishment and goal. Example: the Kinks, "Tired of Waiting for You" (intro and verse 1).

### Aeolian

- **Subtonic shuttle**, minor tonic: ♭VII–i. Example: Gotye, "Somebody That I Used to Know" (intro and verses).
- **Aeolian shuttle** i–♭VII–♭VI–♭VII: a shuttle between i and ♭VI with a passing ♭VII. Being circular, it implies no cadence. Example: Gotye, "Somebody That I Used to Know" (chorus).
- **Aeolian cadence** ♭VI–♭VII–i. It is goal-oriented and not looped. It very often has a picardy third (all major: ♭VI–♭VII–I) and connotes success or heroism. Also called the "Mario cadence." Examples: Howard Shore, Fellowship theme (*The Lord of the Rings*); the Super Mario Bros. level-end fanfare; *Final Fantasy* fanfares.
- **Aeolian lament** i–♭VII–♭VI–v: no leading tone. Example: Eva Simons, "I Don't Like You."

### Dorian

- **Dorian shuttle** i–IV (major IV with minor tonic). It prolongs the tonic. The upper voices of IV form a neighbor figure stressing *la*. An expanded form is i–IV–i⁷–IV.
- Listeners unfamiliar with funk or disco may mishear it as ii–V.
- Examples: CHIC, "Dance, Dance, Dance"; Daft Punk, "Get Lucky" (B dorian, IV–i every four bars).

### Lydian (rare in pop)

- **Lydian shuttle** I–II♯. II♯ acts as a dominant, returning to I through the half step *fi–sol* (↑4̂–5̂). It is usually neutralized later by IV or ii, so the song stays major with a brief lydian color. Fleetwood Mac, "Sara," expands it with Imaj7 and a tonic pedal under II♯, then neutralizes it with a doo-wop schema.
- A false positive: two major chords a step apart can be IV–V or ♭VII–I. Taylor Swift, "Starlight" (A–B) is IV–V in E, which the chorus confirms with a doo-wop rotation.
- **Lydian cadence** II♯–IV–I. *fi* is immediately replaced by *fa*, giving smooth chromatic voice leading. Example: Cee Lo Green, "Forget You" (looped; the background vocals carry the chromatic line).

### Mode identification (ch13 Example 16 flowchart)

| Step | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tonic triad major or minor? | major → {major, mixolydian, lydian}; minor → {minor, aeolian, dorian} |
| 2 | 7̂ = *te* with major tonic? | yes → **mixolydian** |
| 2 | 7̂ = *ti* with minor tonic? | yes → **minor** (harmonic/melodic) |
| 3 | major tonic: *fi* present? | yes → **lydian**; no → **major** |
| 3 | minor tonic: *la* present? | yes → **dorian**; no → **aeolian** |

## Pentatonic Harmony

- The pentatonic is a five-note subset of the diatonic: five consecutive fifths (C–G–D–A–E) with no semitones. All five rotations differ, and pop mainly uses the "major" and "minor" pentatonic rotations.
- In rock the pentatonic usually supplies chord roots. The chords may be major, minor, power chords or anything else, so the total pitch content is often not pentatonic.
- Major ("barre") triads on pentatonic roots create scale-degree conflicts (cross relations) such as *mi/me*, *la/le* and *ti/te*. Example: Stevie Wonder, "Higher Ground" (bass clearly pentatonic, chord quality ambiguous).
- Fifth-based progressions fit the pentatonic. Pairs of fifths a tone apart give D–A–C–G (TLC, "Waterfalls"), which also reads as I–V–♭VII–IV, a double plagal with an added V.
- "Hey Joe" (in E: C–G–D–A–E, ♭VI–♭III–♭VII–IV–I) uses all five pentatonic notes as roots (E–G–A–C–D: 1̂–↓3̂–4̂–↓6̂–↓7̂). Its major triads produce G/G♯ (*me/mi*) and C/C♯ (*le/la*). It also reads as aeolian (the roots lack 2̂ and 5̂) and as an extended double plagal.
- Pentatonic and modal readings often coexist. Analyze separately the overall tonic, the chord roots, the pitch content of the chords, and the pitch content of the whole piece. Then weigh style and genre. Both readings may stand.

## Fragile, Absent and Emergent Tonics (Spicer 2017)

| Type | Technique | Typical lyric association | Example (v2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fragile | Tonic present but weakened, e.g. mostly in inversion; vi⁶ stands where I is expected | loneliness, vulnerability | Sufjan Stevens, "Oh God Where Are You Now?" (G♭ mostly I⁶; E♭m⁶); Elton John, "Somebody Saved My Life Tonight" |
| Emergent | Tonic withheld until a triumphant moment (often chorus). Earlier sections suggest the relative minor or another tonic | triumph, overcoming | "Let It Go" (*Frozen*); Prince, "Little Red Corvette" (B♭ minor → D♭) |
| Absent | Tonic never sounds. It is implied by the melody and a conventional progression | disconnection | Katy Perry, "Last Friday Night" (IV–ii–vi–V in F♯; melody *mi–re–do*) |

"Let It Go," as v2 reads it:
- Intro and verse: Fm–D♭–E♭–B♭(m), first heard as F aeolian (i–VI–VII–iv).
- Prechorus: E♭–D♭, first heard as E♭ mixolydian (I–♭VII).
- Chorus: A♭–E♭–Fm–D♭, where A♭ emerges as tonic (I–V–vi–IV, singer/songwriter).
- In retrospect the verse is vi–IV–V–ii and the prechorus is V–IV, which delays tonic (classic prechorus function). A section-by-section key reading is also acceptable.

## Constraints

1. Every schema chord belongs to its stated mode: mixolydian (double plagal, subtonic shuttle), aeolian (subtonic, aeolian shuttle, aeolian cadence, aeolian lament), dorian (i–IV), lydian (I–II♯). The documented outside chords are iv in the plagal sigh, ♭VI and ♭III in the extended plagal, the picardy I, III♯, V in the lament, and IV in the lydian cadence.
2. Puff = I–iii–IV with minor iii in root position. Major III♯ is a variant, not the prototype.
3. Aeolian shuttle = i–♭VII–♭VI–♭VII. The two-chord i–♭VII is the subtonic shuttle (aeolian version).
4. Doo-wop approaches I by V, singer/songwriter by IV, and hopscotch by vi.
5. Puff and aeolian cadence are goal-directed and not looped. Four-chord schemas and shuttles are typically looped.
6. Lament bass = *do–te–le–sol*. Use v instead of V for an aeolian lament.
7. Name the two-chord shuttle by the tonic quality: major I with ♭VII → mixolydian; minor i with ♭VII → aeolian; minor i with major IV → dorian; I with II♯ → lydian, only if II♯ does not go to V and is not IV–V in another key.
8. ♭VII has dominant function, and so does II♯ in the lydian shuttle (*fi*→*sol*). ♭VI and dorian IV have subdominant function.
9. Singer/songwriter, hopscotch and circle of fifths are tonally ambiguous between relative keys. Decide the tonic from context and melody, not from the loop.
10. Identify mode by tonic quality → 7̂ → *fi* or *la* (flowchart table).
