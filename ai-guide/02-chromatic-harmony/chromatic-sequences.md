# Chromatic Sequences

## Summary

A chromatic sequence keeps both the size and the quality of the transposition interval, and the chord qualities, identical in every copy. So it leaves the key, and its strong-beat roots divide the octave equally. A chromaticized diatonic sequence only adds applied chords or chromatic embellishments, and its root trajectory still follows the scale steps of one key.

## Diatonic vs. Chromaticized vs. Chromatic

| Property | Diatonic | Chromaticized diatonic | Chromatic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transposition interval | Same size, quality varies (stays in key) | Same as diatonic | Same size **and** quality |
| Chord qualities across copies | Vary with scale degree | Vary; applied chords inserted | Identical in model and every copy |
| First chord of model/copies | Diatonic | Diatonic | Contains altered scale degrees |
| Strong-beat roots | Scale steps of the key | Still scale steps (e.g. G–F♯–E–D in G) | Equal division of the octave (usually whole-tone) |
| Tonal result | Stays in key | Stays in key | Ambiguous tonic; distant modulation or temporary disruption |

Test: list the roots on successive strong beats. If they follow the key's scale steps, the sequence is chromaticized, not chromatic. "Contains chromatic pitches" is not enough to call it chromatic.

Shared rule for every type: voice leading is identical in every voice in every copy, and corresponding chords use the same voicing.

## Sequence Types

Diatonic context (from `v2/03-diatonic-harmony/14`): descending fifths (two-chord model, copy down by step), descending thirds (Pachelbel; copy down by third), ascending 5–6 (ascending step; copy up by step).

| Type (v2 name) | Diatonic root motion | Chromatic root motion | Model → copy interval (chromatic) | Chord qualities (chromatic) | Voice leading | Example (pcs verified) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Descending fifths | ↓5th/↑4th; one ↓A4 (e.g. IV–vii° in major) to stay in key | Every root ↓P5 | ↓M2 | Every chord a dominant seventh, each V7 of the next | Elided resolution: 3rd (leading tone) ↓ semitone → new 7th; 7th ↓ semitone → new 3rd | G7–C7–F7–B♭7–E♭7–A♭7; strong-beat roots G–F–E♭ (whole-tone) |
| Ascending 5–6 (chromatic ascending step) | ↓3rd, ↑P4, with one inconsistent interval (e.g. D–B♭, a M3) | ↓m3, ↑P4 throughout | ↑M2 | Strong beats major triads; weak beats dominant sevenths (V7 of next) | Applied V7 resolves to the next strong-beat triad | B♭–G7–C–A7–D–B7–E; E treated as V → A major (half step below the start) |
| Descending 5–6 (descending thirds / Pachelbel) | ↓P4, ↑M2 or m2 | ↓P4, ↑m3 | ↓M2 | All major triads | Weak-beat chords in 6/3 give a bass descending by semitone | D–A–C–G–B♭–F–A♭–E♭–F♯–C♯–E–B–D; bass with weak 6/3: D–C♯–C–B–B♭–… ; strong beats whole-tone |
| Parallel 6/3 | Stepwise chain of 6/3 chords | Same-quality 6/3 chords ↓ semitone | ↓m2 (one-chord model) | All major triads in first inversion | Only sequence without a two-chord model; in four voices one voice must leap to avoid parallel octaves; often a chain of 7–6 suspensions | Chopin, Impromptu in A♭: major 6/3 chords ↓ semitone until ii, then HC |
| Omnibus | — | Chromatic voice exchange, see below | Resets every 4 chords on a V7 a m3 away | V7, minor 6/4, V7/Ger+6 | Bass vs. one upper voice in contrary semitones; other voices held | G7–B♭⁴₂–Dm⁶₄–B♭7–G⁶₅ |

Notes:
- The chromatic descending-fifths sequence is so tonally ambiguous that Roman numerals are nominal; v2 adds lead-sheet symbols.
- A chromatic descending 5–6 can stop after one copy to modulate (D major → C major), or run a full octave and return to a tonic that hardly sounds like one. Schubert uses it as tonic prolongation.
- Chromatic parallel 6/3 still prolongs tonic through to the predominant, but is less tonally stable than the diatonic form.

## Variants of the Chromatic Descending-Fifths Sequence

| Variant | Construction | Result | v2 repertoire |
|---|---|---|---|
| V⁴₃–V7 alternation | Invert every other dominant seventh | Smoother bass | — |
| Diminished-seventh substitution | Replace each V7 with the vii°7 that keeps its leading tone; alternate applied vii°⁴₂ and vii°⁶₅ | Bass descends by semitone; heard as a chromatic descent of dim7 chords (symmetry). Any chord can pivot to a new key | Bach (F minor → G minor, then HC) |
| V7 with ♭5 | Lower the 5th of each dominant seventh; first chord of each model in ⁴₃ (♭5 in bass) | Chromatic bass descending by semitone | Berg, "Schlafend trägt man mich" (root position, non-sequential, lead-sheet analysis) |
| Fr+6 → V7 pairs | Read each V7♭5 as an enharmonic French sixth: C7♭5 (C–E–G♭–B♭) = Fr+6 of B♭ (G♭–B♭–C–E) → F7 = V of B♭ | Each model = Fr+6 → V7 in a key; keys fall by whole step (B♭, A♭, …) | — |
| Ger+6 → V7 pairs | Swap the Fr+6 for a Ger+6: G♭7 (= Ger+6 of B♭) → F7 → E7 (= Ger+6 of A♭) → E♭7 | Enharmonically a chain of dominant sevenths descending by semitone; parallel fifths between every pair | Chopin, Mazurka in C♯ minor, Op. 30 No. 4, mm. 124–39 |

## The Omnibus

A chromatic voice exchange in contrary motion between the bass and one upper voice, by semitones. It is usually a dominant prolongation, and can also modulate to distant keys. Only the two exchanging voices move; the others are common tones.

### Converging (bass ↑, leading tone ↓)

From a root-position V7, here G7 (V7 in C). Held: the 5th and 7th (D, F).

| Chord | Bass | Leading-tone voice | Held | Chord (pcs) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | G | B | D, F | G7 | Root-position V7 |
| 2 | G♯/A♭ | B♭ | D, F | B♭7, 7th in bass | Variant: bass moves alone → G♯–B–D–F (dim7, 3 common tones with chord 1) |
| 3 | A | A | D, F | D minor 6/4 | Voices meet |
| 4 | B♭ | A♭/G♯ | D, F | B♭7 = Ger+6 (B♭–D–F–G♯) | Root-position V7 three semitones on |
| 5 | B | G | D, F | G7/B (V⁶₅) | Original V7, first inversion (four semitones on) |

- Chords 2–4 imply the key a whole step above (here D: i⁶₄, Ger+6). Brahms (Scherzo Op. 4) prolongs V of B, then later V of C♯, the key implied by the inner chords.
- Reset: skip chord 5, reinterpret chord 4 as a new V7, and exchange its leading tone with the bass. Roots of the successive V7s rise by minor thirds: G7 → B♭7 → D♭7 → E7 → G7 (four iterations = full octave, rare in practice).
- Chopin, Nocturne Op. 27 No. 2 runs two iterations with the dim7 variant as chord 2: G♯7 (C♯ minor) → B7 → D7 (G major).

### Diverging (bass ↓, 7th ↑)

From G7. Held: the 3rd and 5th (B, D).

| Step | Bass | 7th voice | Chord (pcs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | G | F | G7 |
| 2 | F♯ | F♯ | B minor 6/4 |
| 3 | F | G | G7/F (V⁴₂) |
| 4 | E | G♯ | E7, root position: new V7 |

- Reset on E7 (new seventh D against bass E): V7 roots fall by minor thirds, G7 → E7 → C♯7 → B♭7 → G7.
- This is the converging pattern in reverse, read from its chord 4. To get an exact reversal, put chord 5 (a first-inversion V7, e.g. E7/G♯) before any chord 4 (e.g. G7); Schubert's Piano Sonata excerpt starts this way.
- Mussorgsky uses two iterations to modulate to E major.

## Equal Divisions of the Octave

Only five intervals divide one octave equally. Diatonic progressions divide it unequally (the tritone in descending fifths, the half steps in step sequences), and that asymmetry is what pulls toward a tonic. Equal division removes dominant-to-tonic motion, so it gives tonal ambiguity.

| Root interval | Roots per octave | Roots outline | Related collection | Where it appears (v2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| m2 | 12 | Chromatic scale | — | Chromatic parallel 6/3 (Chopin); Ger+6 → V7 chain (V7s ↓ semitone). v2 calls this "somewhat trivial" |
| M2 | 6 | Whole-tone scale | Whole-tone | Strong beats of chromatic descending-fifths, ascending 5–6 and descending 5–6 sequences |
| m3 | 4 | Diminished seventh chord | Octatonic (overlapping dim7s) | Usually each root tonicized or separated by intervening chords, more rarely in immediate succession; omnibus resets; major triads of an RP cycle (C–A–F♯–E♭) |
| M3 | 3 | Augmented triad (C–E–G♯/A♭–C) | Hexatonic (two augmented triads) | Both immediate and with intervening chords; major triads of a PL cycle (C–A♭–E) |
| TT | 2 | — | — | Only two chords, trivial |

Roots that move consistently by P4/P5 also blur the key, but they do not close within one octave.

## Constraints

1. Call a sequence chromatic only if the transposition interval keeps size and quality and every copy repeats the model's chord qualities. Applied chords alone make it chromaticized.
2. Keep the same voice leading and voicing in every copy, in every voice.
3. Chromatic descending fifths with all dominant sevenths: use elided resolutions. The leading tone goes down a semitone to the next 7th, and the 7th goes down a semitone to the next leading tone.
4. Chromatic ascending 5–6: change any root interval that breaks the ↓m3/↑P4 pattern (e.g. B♭7 → B7, then E♭ → E), and make every strong-beat triad major.
5. Chromatic descending 5–6: roots ↓P4 then ↑m3, so the copy falls a whole step; put weak-beat chords in 6/3 for a chromatic bass.
6. Parallel 6/3 in four voices: let one voice leap so the chain avoids parallel octaves.
7. Omnibus: move only the bass and the exchanging voice, by semitones in contrary motion. Converging exchanges root and leading tone; diverging exchanges root and seventh.
8. Restart an omnibus only on a root-position V7 (chord 4). Converging resets move V7 roots up m3, diverging resets move them down m3.
9. The Ger+6 → V7 chain makes parallel fifths. Use it only where that is acceptable, as Chopin does.
