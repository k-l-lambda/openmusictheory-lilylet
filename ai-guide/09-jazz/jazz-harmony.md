# Jazz Harmony

## Summary

Jazz harmony is written as absolute chord symbols (root, triad quality, extensions, bass note) and organized around the ii–V–I schema. Progressions are elaborated by inserting chords (applied V7, applied ii7, CT°7) or replacing them (applied-dominant, mixture and tritone substitutions). Chord-scale theory maps each chord to a mode for improvisation.

## Chord-Symbol Grammar

### Four components and default qualities

A symbol gives (1) root, (2) triad quality, (3) extensions beyond the triad, (4) a non-root bass note after a slash. Unmarked parts take defaults:

| Element | Default when unmarked |
|---|---|
| Triad | major |
| 7 | minor seventh (so C7 = dominant seventh) |
| 9, 11, 13, added tones | major (9, 13) or perfect (11) |
| Bass | root position |

Symbols are not standardized. v2 uses the first form in each row below and the sharp/flat system for alterations (a plus/minus system also exists; pick one and stay consistent).

### Triads and seventh chords (root C)

| Quality | Symbols (v2's first) | Pitches |
|---|---|---|
| major | C | C E G |
| minor | Cmi, Cm, C- | C E♭ G |
| diminished | C°, Cdim, Cm(♭5), Cm(-5) | C E♭ G♭ |
| augmented | C+, Caug | C E G♯ |
| dominant seventh | C7 | C E G B♭ |
| major seventh | Cma7, Cmaj7, C∆7 | C E G B |
| minor seventh | Cmi7, Cm7, C-7 | C E♭ G B♭ |
| half-diminished seventh | Cø7, Cm7♭5, C-7♭5 | C E♭ G♭ B♭ |
| diminished seventh | C°7 | C E♭ G♭ B𝄫 |
| other (named in v2) | Cm(maj7), Caug7 | C E♭ G B; C E G♯ B♭ |

### Extensions: what each symbol implies

An extension implies every extension below it. Implied is not the same as played: voicings usually omit some of them (see [Jazz Voicings](jazz-voicings.md)).

| Symbol | Implied pitches (root C) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| C9 | C E G B♭ D | 9 implies the minor 7th |
| C11 | C E G B♭ D F | 11 implies 9 and 7 |
| C13 | C E G B♭ D F A | 13 implies 11, 9, 7; only the 7th is minor. Implied ≠ played: intermediate extensions are usually omitted in voicings (v2 06/02 n. 1; see [Jazz Voicings](jazz-voicings.md)) |
| Cadd9 | C E G D | "add" = no seventh; D voiced above the triad |
| Cadd2 | C D E G | added tone voiced inside the triad |
| C6 | C E G A | added sixth, written without "add" |
| C6/9 | C E G A D | sixth and ninth, written without "add" |
| Csus | C F G | sus = 3rd replaced by P4 above root (default) |
| Csus2 | C D G | 3rd replaced by M2 |
| C7sus | C F G B♭ | "sus" comes after the extension (Csus7 would look like Csus2) |

### Alterations

- ♯ / ♭ on an extension raise / lower the default interval by a half step. They are relative to the root, not literal accidentals: E7(♭9) adds F♮; B♭7(♯11) adds E♮.
- C7(♯11) = C E G B♭ F♯ (F♯ replaces the default F). C7(♭9) = C E G B♭ D♭.
- Always write 7 before altered extensions. Put alterations in parentheses; separate several with slashes, e.g. C7(♭9/♯11) = C E G B♭ D♭ F♯.

### Slash chords

- X/Y = chord X over bass Y. C/E = C triad with E in the bass.
- The bass need not belong to the chord: C/F♯ = C triad over F♯.
- Chord symbols are performance shorthand, not analysis. C/A♭ (pitches A♭ C E G) is often preferred to the functionally descriptive A♭ma7(♯5) (same pitches). Neither is always right; choose the easier one to read in context.

### Chord symbols vs. Roman numerals

Chord symbols are absolute (exact notes, no key). Roman numerals are relative to a key. Read symbols without key-relative assumptions.

## ii–V–I

### Prototypes (in C)

| Mode | Roman numerals | Qualities | Chords in C |
|---|---|---|---|
| major | ii7 – V7 – Ima7 | mi7 – 7 – ma7 | Dmi7 – G7 – Cma7 |
| minor | iiø7 – V7 – i7 | ø7 – 7 – mi7 | Dø7 – G7 – Cmi7 |

V is dominant-quality in both modes. Identify the schema by root motion by descending fifth plus the quality sequence. A ii–V alone (no I) is still recognized as the schema.

### Recognizable variants (v2 examples)

| Change | Example in C | Tune cited in v2 |
|---|---|---|
| Ima7 → I6 | Dmi7 – G7 – C6 | "Misty", "My Funny Valentine" |
| V7 → augmented V (minor 7th kept) | Dmi7 – G+7 (G B D♯ F) – C | "Prelude to a Kiss" |
| V7 with lowered fifth | Dmi7 – G7(♭5) (G B D♭ F) – C | "A Night in Tunisia" |

### Applied ii–V's

- Dominant function is marked by quality (dominant seventh; also fully diminished seventh). Jazz extends applied chords to applied ii chords: a whole ii–V–I can tonicize any chord.
- A ii–V need not resolve to its I to be heard as a ii–V.
- Sequenced ii–V–I's ("Afternoon in Paris", in C): Cma7 → Cmi7 F7 B♭ma7 (♭VII) → B♭mi7 E♭7 A♭ma7 (♭VI). Turning each Ima7 into a mi7 (same root) makes it the next ii7 and moves the key down a whole step.
- McClimon's ii–V space (v2 04, Ex. 5) relates chords by three moves: root down a fifth; ma7 → 7 (lower the seventh); 7 → mi7 (lower the third).

## Turnarounds

A turnaround loops back to the tonic. Base form: I – vi – ii – V – I.

| Variant | Roman numerals | In C |
|---|---|---|
| diatonic | I – vi7 – ii7 – V7 – I | Cma7 – Ami7 – Dmi7 – G7 – Cma7 |
| applied V of ii (same root as vi) | I – V7/ii – ii7 – V7 – I | Cma7 – A7 – Dmi7 – G7 – Cma7 |
| tonicized ii (ii/ii – V/ii – ii) | I – ii7/ii – V7/ii – ii7 – V7 – I | Cma7 – Emi7 – A7 – Dmi7 – G7 – Cma7 |

## Embellishing (Inserted) Chords

Embellishing chords are added to a progression; substitutions replace a chord. Inserting a chord always changes the harmonic rhythm: split an existing chord's duration and give part of it to the new chord.

| Insertion | Rule | v2 example ("Mood Indigo": B♭ – C7 – Cmi7 – F7 – B♭) |
|---|---|---|
| Applied V7 | Any chord can be preceded by its applied V7. | Last two beats of B♭ become G7 (V7 of C7). |
| Applied ii7 | Precede a dominant-quality chord with the mi7 or ø7 chord a fifth above it, creating a ii–V. | First half of C7 becomes Gmi7: Gmi7 – C7 – Cmi7 – F7 (two ii–V's in a row). |
| CT°7 | Hold the root of the embellished chord; move all other voices by step to the °7 chord containing that root. | CT°7 on B♭ = C♯ E G B♭; on C = D♯ F♯ A C. |

CT°7 rules:
- The root of the chord of resolution is always a member of the CT°7.
- Placement: in the middle of the prolonged harmony (I – CT°7 – I), or in place of its first statement (CT°7 – I).
- It is a voice-leading chord (neighbor motion), not a scale-degree harmony. v2 gives it no Roman numeral and no chord symbol in analyses; it is often improvised rather than written.

Lead-sheet notation (insertions and substitutions alike): not notated (improvised), written into the chord symbols, or given in parentheses as an optional reharmonization.

## Substitutions

| Substitution | Replaces | With | In C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Applied-dominant | first chord of a root-motion-by-fifth pair | dominant seventh on the same root | turnaround Cma7 – Ami7 – Dmi7 – G7 → Cma7 – A7 – D7 – G7 (chain of applied V7s) |
| Mode mixture | diatonic chord | parallel-minor version; same function, new color | ii7 → iiø7 (Dmi7 → Dø7); V7 → V7(♭9) (G7 → G7(♭9) = G B D F A♭) |
| Tritone | a V7 chord, applied or diatonic | dominant seventh a tritone away | G7 → D♭7: Dmi7 – D♭7 – Cma7 |

Mixture: the most common jazz mixture chords are iiø7 and V7(♭9). Both substitute le for la (A♭ for A in C). Mixture works best when the inflected degree is la → le. Built-in mixture example: "All of You" uses Fmi (iv) and Dø7 in C major.

### Tritone substitution: scope

- It replaces dominant seventh chords only (diatonic V7 or applied V7). Any dominant seventh can be replaced by the dominant seventh a tritone away, and the progression keeps its function.
- Both chords share the same tritone: G7 has B–F, D♭7 (D♭ F A♭ C♭) has F–C♭(=B).
- Not every V7 should be replaced: v2 says it sounds best when the V7 resolves to I rather than moving to another chord. The sub then resolves down by minor second (D♭7 → C). That chromatic bass descent is the most reliable way to recognize one.
- A tritone-substituted V7 can be preceded by its own ii7 (A♭mi7 – D♭7 – C), per McClimon's "Blues for Alice" analysis.
- Applied to the turnaround: first make every chord dominant (C – A7 – D7 – G7), then replace every other one with its tritone sub, which yields a chromatic bass:
  - C – E♭7 – D7 – D♭7 – C (bass C E♭ D D♭ C)
  - C – A7 – A♭7 – G7 – C (bass C A A♭ G C)

## Chord-Scale Theory

A thirteenth chord's seven notes, rearranged stepwise, form a seven-note scale. Dmi13 (D F A C E G B) = D dorian.

### By Roman numeral (major key, in C)

| Roman numeral | Mode | Example | 13th-chord stack |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ima7 | ionian | Cma7 = C ionian | C E G B D F A |
| ii7 | dorian | Dmi7 = D dorian | D F A C E G B |
| iii7 | phrygian | Emi7 = E phrygian | E G B D F A C |
| IVma7 | lydian | Fma7 = F lydian | F A C E G B D |
| V7 | mixolydian | G7 = G mixolydian | G B D F A C E |
| vi7 | aeolian | Ami7 = A aeolian | A C E G B D F |
| viiø7 | locrian | Bø7 = B locrian | B D F A C E G |

### By chord quality

| Chord quality | Scale(s) |
|---|---|
| minor seventh | dorian (ii), phrygian (iii), aeolian (vi) |
| major seventh | ionian (I), lydian (IV) |
| dominant seventh | mixolydian |
| half-diminished seventh | locrian |

Basic ii–V–I set: mi7 = dorian, 7 = mixolydian, ma7 = ionian. Chord-scales from melodic minor, harmonic minor and harmonic major exist, but v2 defers them to its further reading.

Usage rules:
- Choose the scale from the chord's Roman numeral in the prevailing key, not from its quality alone (vi7 = aeolian, not dorian; IVma7 = lydian, not ionian). Chord-scales are not key changes.
- Put chord tones on downbeats. The other three scale notes act as passing tones (2, 4, 6) or extensions (9, 11, 13).
- Limitations noted in v2: no voice leading between chords; misses bebop and blues chromaticism (neighbor and passing tones, secondary leading tones, blue notes); anachronistic for 1920–50 repertoire.

## Constraints

1. Unmarked triad = major; unmarked 7 = minor seventh; unmarked 9/11/13 = major/perfect.
2. 9, 11 or 13 implies all lower extensions down to the 7th. "add", 6 and 6/9 imply no seventh.
3. sus replaces the 3rd with P4 (sus2: M2). Write it after the extension (C7sus).
4. ♯/♭ on an extension alter the interval above the root, not the letter's accidental. Write 7 before alterations and use parentheses.
5. Slash bass may be a non-chord tone.
6. ii–V–I: mi7–7–ma7 (major), ø7–7–mi7 (minor); roots fall by fifths; V is always dominant-quality.
7. An applied ii7 goes before a dominant-quality chord: mi7 or ø7 on the root a fifth above.
8. A CT°7 must contain the root of the chord it embellishes; every other voice moves by step.
9. Inserting an embellishing chord requires re-dividing the harmonic rhythm.
10. A tritone sub replaces only a dominant seventh (diatonic or applied) with the dominant seventh a tritone away; it works best where the original V7 resolves to I, giving a half-step bass descent.
11. Mixture substitutes are most idiomatic when they turn la into le (iiø7, V7(♭9)).
12. Pick a chord-scale from function (Roman numeral), not quality alone; land on chord tones on downbeats.
